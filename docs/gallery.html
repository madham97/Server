Reading heat in a rat enclosure

A camera on a Raspberry Pi photographs a rodent enclosure and, at the same moment, records an 80 × 62 pixel temperature map. Below are real captures from that device — the photograph on the left, the temperature on the right. The boxes are drawn by hand, by a person, on the photograph.

cooler surfacewarmer surface

Why the temperature maps looked broken

Each frame is stored as a number from 0 to 255 stretched across a fixed range of 10 °C to 45 °C. A cool enclosure at night only occupies about 20–26 °C of that range, so every value lands in the bottom fifth and the picture comes out almost black. The sensor was working the whole time; the way we were drawing it was not.

Same frame, same data, both sides

Nothing was added on the right. The numbers were converted back to degrees and then spread across the range this particular frame actually covers.

A keeper, not a rat

Hands are warm too. What separates them is size and shape — broad and diffuse rather than a compact blob — not temperature. Heat alone does not say what something is.

And where it stops working

photograph · 16:12temperature

On a hot afternoon the contrast inverts

By mid-afternoon the bedding has been sitting in the sun and is warmer than the animals on it. The same rat that glowed white at dawn now reads colder than its surroundings. Anything that simply looks for the hottest thing in the frame fails here.

surroundings 36.9 °C animal 34.6 °C difference −2.3 °C

Two more honest limits. The brightest thing in a night frame is often the camera's own infrared lamp reflecting off a basket, not an animal. And the photograph and the temperature map are taken one after another rather than at the same instant, so a moving animal can sit in slightly different places in the two panels.
